*Same brief, derived from scratch: a novel approach that could actually make money, any strategy, any API, not limited to Robinhood data. I deliberately did not re-read the prior memos before reasoning; a short overlap note for your cross-model comparison is at the end.*

---

## 0. My starting question

Not "what strategy should the bot run" but: **what is the cheapest thing in the world right now that the market still pays for?**

In 2026 the cheapest thing in the world is *reading*. A model can read every filing, every foreign disclosure, every tender document, every other model's output, for pennies. So the honest version of the question becomes: **where does the market still pay a reader?** Not a forecaster — forecasting is the most over-supplied skill in finance. A *reader*.

I found four places, and they form a portfolio ordered by certainty. The unifying name I'd give the whole thing: **Second Reader** — every sleeve makes money by reading something carefully that everyone else reads late, lazily, in the wrong language, or not at all. Nothing below requires predicting a price. Each sleeve has a measurable intermediate quantity you can validate *before* risking dollars, an explicit falsifier, and a reason the edge is retail-sized (i.e., why funds haven't eaten it).

One reflexive observation drives the most novel piece: **as of May 27, 2026, Robinhood points thousands of retail LLM agents at the market.** Most people see a product. I see a new, *predictable* order flow — generated by publicly purchasable models that you can query yourself. More on that in Sleeve 3.

## Sleeve 0 — Structural crumbs (near-arbitrage, tiny, real)

**The idea:** Some corporate actions hand small holders free money by statute or by deal mechanics, because processing small shareholders costs companies more than paying them off. The canonical one: **odd-lot tender priority** — tenders that accept holders of ≤99 shares *without proration*. Buy 99 shares after announcement below the tender price, tender, collect the spread. Returns of 5–18% on capital in ~a month, with deal-break as the only real risk. These still exist in 2026 (active trackers list deals through this year) and they are *structurally retail-only*: a fund cannot deploy meaningful capital into a 99-share cap, so nobody competes the spread away.

Adjacent crumbs in the same family: under-subscribed Dutch tenders, going-private cash-outs for sub-threshold holders, cash-in-lieu round-ups after splits/mergers.

**Why an LLM:** the work is reading SC TO-I / 13E-4 / DEF 14A filings for odd-lot clauses, proration terms, and withdrawal conditions the moment they hit EDGAR — exactly the kind of dull, precise reading a model does for free and humans do via $200/yr newsletters.

**Honest sizing:** 1–5 opportunities a year, $50–$500 each. This sleeve will not make you rich. It's in the portfolio because it is the only component that is *almost certainly* +EV, it's uncorrelated with everything, and it forces you to build the EDGAR-reading muscle the other sleeves reuse.

**Falsifier:** none needed — each deal is individually verifiable from the filing. Skip any deal where the spread is under 3% or the acquirer has a withdrawal history.

## Sleeve 1 — Overreaction triage (the engine)

**The idea:** When a stock drops 10–25% intraday, the crash is one of two species: *informed* (the news genuinely destroys that much value — fraud, guidance collapse, trial failure) or *mechanical/misread* (headline scarier than the document, sympathy selloff, forced/index selling, a downgrade that contains no new facts, a dilutive-looking offering by a healthy company). The literature on short-term reversal after non-fundamental shocks is old and robust; the reason it persists is that separating the species *within the first hour* requires actually reading the source document fast — which is precisely an LLM's comparative advantage. Humans panic-sell first and read later; the bot reads first.

**The trade:** monitor for ≥10% intraday drops in liquid-enough names ($300M–$10B cap, >$1M ADV). On trigger, pull the *primary source* (the 8-K, the court order, the downgrade note's substance, the offering prospectus) — never the headline. Score against a hard rubric:

1. Does the news mechanically change EPS, cash flow, or solvency? By how much, in dollars?
2. Is the drawdown proportional to that dollar number, or a multiple of it?
3. Is this a binary-resolution situation (clinical trial, fraud allegation, going concern)? → **automatic skip.** Binary crashes are usually correctly priced; this sleeve only buys *graded* overreactions.
4. Is there forced-flow context (index exclusion, fund liquidation, margin cascade)? → strongest setups.

Buy only when the rubric says "non-fundamental or disproportionate," hold 2–10 days for partial reversion, hard stop at −8%, time-stop at 10 days, position ≤3% of account, max 2 concurrent.

**Why retail-sized:** event-driven funds run this on big names; in the $300M–$3B band, coverage is thin and the panic flow is dominated by retail and undiscriminating systematic sellers.

**Falsifier:** over the first 40 paper signals, if reversion hit-rate ≤ 55% or average net return ≤ 0 after a 0.3% cost haircut, the rubric isn't separating the species — kill or rebuild.

## Sleeve 2 — The polyglot desk (the LLM-native moat)

**The idea:** Hundreds of foreign small/mid caps trade in the US as ADRs or F-shares. Their *material disclosures drop overnight in Japanese, Korean, Mandarin, German, Portuguese* — on TDnet, DART, MOPS, DGAP — hours before any English-language coverage exists. For mega-caps (Toyota, Samsung suppliers), global desks price this instantly. For a $600M Japanese industrial with an ADR and *zero* English analyst coverage, the US line frequently doesn't fully react until English media or a translated note catches up — a documented lag that widens as English coverage thins.

A 2026 LLM reads Japanese disclosure PDFs as natively as English ones. This is the purest expression of "the market still pays a reader": the edge isn't intelligence, it's *literacy plus being awake at 3am*, both of which cost you fractions of a cent.

**The trade:** maintain a mapped universe (OpenFIGI: local line ↔ US line) of US-listed foreign names under ~$5B with <3 English news items/month. Poll local disclosure feeds during their business day (US night). For each filing: translate, classify materiality and direction, check whether the *local* line moved (it usually does — that's your ground truth, a luxury Sleeve 1 doesn't have: **the home market grades your read before you trade**). If local moved ≥X% on the news and the US line's pre-market hasn't tracked it, enter at the US open with limits, exit over 1–5 days as English coverage saturates.

**Why retail-sized:** the names are too small and too scattered across too many languages for a fund to staff, and the per-name capacity is tiny. It's a breadth game — exactly what a model has and an analyst team doesn't.

**Falsifier:** measurable *without trading*: across 60 days of logged signals, does "local line moved ≥3%, US line gap < half of that" predict US open-to-+3-day convergence? If the US line tracks the local move pre-market more than ~80% of the time, the lag is gone for your universe — shrink to even less-covered names or kill.

**Constraint to respect:** Robinhood agentic beta is long-only equities — so trade only the long side of the lag (local good news, US line flat). The short side waits for a margin venue.

## Sleeve 3 — Reading the readers (the 2026-novel bet)

This is the idea I'd be most excited about, and the one I'd validate most cautiously.

**The premise:** Robinhood just connected a mass of retail LLM agents to live brokerage accounts — and beyond Robinhood, millions of people now ask ChatGPT/Claude/Grok "what stock should I buy?" and act on it. Academic work already finds chatbot-driven attention measurably shifts retail trading activity and liquidity. Here is the structural quirk: **this flow is generated by a small number of publicly purchasable models.** Unlike human retail flow, which you can only observe after the fact, *agent flow can be simulated in advance* — by literally running the same models with the same retail-typical prompts and watching what they say.

That makes it the newest member of the most reliable family of edges in market history: **predictable flow** (index reconstitution, dividend reinvestment, options-expiry pinning). You don't have to be smarter than anyone. You just have to know where the money must move before it moves.

**The build — a "synthetic crowd":**
- Daily, at fixed times, query each consumer-facing model (ChatGPT's default model, Claude, Grok, Gemini) with ~10–15 retail prompt archetypes: "what stock should I buy today," "best AI stock under $20," "undervalued stock with catalyst this month," etc., at deployed settings.
- Log every ticker mentioned. Compute a **consensus score** (cross-model, cross-prompt frequency) and a **novelty flag** (ticker newly entering consensus today).
- The tradable hypotheses:
  - **H1 (onset ride):** small/mid-cap names *newly entering* multi-model consensus see abnormal retail buying over the next 1–5 days → long the onset. (Mega-cap mentions are noise — flow is immaterial to AAPL. The signal lives in the tail where models name sub-$10B tickers.)
  - **H2 (saturation fade):** names with sustained high consensus underperform after flow peaks → exit/avoid (long-only: this is a sell-discipline rule, not a short).
  - **H3 (model-release rotation):** when a major default model updates, its recommendation basket rotates discontinuously → a datable, calendar-like flow event. Nobody is trading this yet. Watch one release cycle and measure.

**The discipline — measure before trading:** the synthetic crowd costs ~$3/day of tokens and zero market risk. Run it for 4–8 weeks against free flow proxies (FINRA off-exchange volume files, retail-activity trackers) and answer one question: *does consensus onset predict abnormal retail volume in the tail names?* If yes, start trading H1 small. If no, you've spent $150 to learn something genuinely interesting and you publish the null in your own notes and move on.

**Why this might be nothing:** agentic AUM may still be too small; chatbot picks may cluster too heavily in mega-caps; platforms may randomize/diversify recommendations precisely to prevent this. **Why it might be big:** the signal *grows with adoption* — every quarter more money routes through the same handful of models — and you'd be measuring it from week one of the era. Most edges decay from the moment you find them. This one compounds until the pros notice.

## How the four sleeves fit together

| | Edge type | Certainty | Capital share | LLM's role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Crumbs | Structural / statutory | Very high, tiny | Opportunistic | Read filings for clauses |
| 1. Overreaction triage | Behavioral reversal | Moderate, documented | ~40% | Read source docs for materiality, fast |
| 2. Polyglot desk | Information lag | Moderate, measurable pre-trade | ~40% | Read foreign languages overnight |
| 3. Reading the readers | Predictable flow | Unknown, measurable cheaply | 0% → ≤20% after validation | Read *the other models* |

Shared spine (kept brief — it's standard hygiene, not the idea): positions ≤3%, sector caps, −3% daily / −8% drawdown halts, every signal logged with stated confidence and scored later (Brier), paper → shadow → $50/trade real, slippage tracked against assumption, and **per-sleeve kill criteria enforced as written, not renegotiated after a hot week.**

Data/API stack (~$40–80/mo + tokens): EDGAR full-text + RSS (free), TDnet/DART/MOPS/DGAP feeds (free), OpenFIGI mapping (free), FINRA off-exchange volume (free), Polygon or Tiingo for prices (~$30), model APIs for the synthetic crowd (~$3/day), Robinhood MCP for execution with Alpaca as fallback.

**90-day order of operations:** Sleeve 0 scanner first (one week, immediately useful, builds the EDGAR muscle) → Sleeve 1 paper (the engine; 40-signal falsifier test) → Sleeve 2 measurement harness (no trading; 60-day lag study) → Sleeve 3 synthetic crowd logging in parallel from day one, because its dataset is *time-zero-valuable*: every week you don't log is agent-era history you can never recover.

## What would make me wrong (the whole memo, not just a sleeve)

- If retail-accessible US execution costs on $300M–$3B names exceed ~0.5% round-trip in practice, Sleeves 1–2 compress badly. Only live fills settle this.
- If LLM materiality judgment is systematically overconfident on crashes (plausible — models are trained to be agreeable, and "this panic is overdone" is an agreeable thought), Sleeve 1's rubric needs adversarial reframing ("argue this crash is *correct*" as a mandatory counter-pass).
- If everything above is already productized inside two quant shops I can't see, the lags I'm describing are narrower than the public record suggests. The measurement-first design means you find that out for under $500.
